## Reading the statement, as four checks

The theorem `exact_count_and_sat` states that for every `k >= 2` there exists **one** fixed
deterministic Turing machine and constants `C, A, S, d, e > 0` such that for **every** `n` and
**every** CNF of width at most `k` over `n` variables, the machine halts with the **exact** number
of satisfying assignments in binary, with the SAT answer in the state, within time
`<= C * (bits+1)^d * 2^(A * runtimeScale(n))` and space `<= S * (bits+1)^e` at every prefix of the
run.

| what I looked for | what I found |
|---|---|
| quantifiers in the wrong place | **correct**: machine and constants are fixed before the universal quantifier over `n` and over formulas, so the machine is uniform in `n` |
| an inflated machine model | **more restricted than usual**: the transition function reads only the tape symbol the current state designates, not all of them; deterministic, no oracle, no advice |
| strength of what is proved | **stronger than SAT**: it counts exactly, and it covers every `k >= 2`, so `k = 3` is included |
| an encoding that hides cost | **not established.** The encoding is unary (`encodeNat n = n x true ++ [false]`). I did not establish what that costs in encoding length. Unary encoding of an integer is in general exponential in its binary length, and whether it stays polynomial here depends on the bounds on the encoded integers and on the formula size, which I did not check |

Because of the fourth row, two things remain unestablished and are not claimed anywhere in this
repository: that the formal statement is a faithful rendering of k-SAT, and that the unary encoding
keeps the input size polynomial. An `o(n)` exponent bears on the ETH only once both hold.

## The finding: where the ratio sits above 1

The bound is `2^(A * runtimeScale(n))` with, verbatim from their file,

```
runtimeScale(n) = n * ln(ln(n+16))^2 / sqrt(ln(n+16))
```

Dividing by `n`, the ratio is `ln(L)^2 / sqrt(L)` with `L = ln(n+16)`, which **tends to zero**. That
is what makes the bound `2^(o(n))`. But `o(n)` does not say **when**, and the ratio **rises before
it falls**:

| `n` (order of magnitude) | ratio `runtimeScale(n)/n` | upper bound below `2^n`? |
|---|---|---|
| `10^24` | **2.165** (the maximum) | no |
| `10^434` | 1.509 | no |
| `10^2171` | 1.026 | no |
| `10^2390` | 1.0000237 | **no** |
| **`10^2390.22`** | **0.9999990** | **yes, from here on** |
| `10^43429` | 0.419 | yes |

Measured by [`scripts/runtime_scale_crossover.py`](scripts/runtime_scale_crossover.py), eight
self-tests before any number is printed, plus a `--plant` mode that corrupts the transcribed formula
and requires the self-tests to reject it. Table in
[`scripts/runtime_scale_crossover.csv`](scripts/runtime_scale_crossover.csv).

**The maximum has a closed form and can be checked by hand.** Differentiating `ln(L)^2 / sqrt(L)`
and setting it to zero gives `ln L = 4`: the maximum is at `L = e^4 = 54.598` and equals exactly
`16/e^2 = 2.1653645`. The script's numerical search is checked against that closed form rather than
against itself, so a search with 2% error fails the self-test.

**Both crossings have a closed form too, and that is what makes the second one visible.** With
`u = ln L`, the equation `ln(L)^2 = sqrt(L)` becomes `u^2 = e^(u/2)`, that is `ln u = u/4`, whose
solution is `u = -4 W(-1/4)`. Hence

```
L = exp(-4 W(-1/4))
```

and `-1/4` lies in `(-1/e, 0)`, **where the Lambert W function has two real branches**. So the
equation has **two** crossings, not one:

| branch | `L` | `n` | measured ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| `W_0` (principal) | 4.177077 | **49.175096** | 1.000000000000000 |
| `W_-1` (lower) | 5503.6647 | `10^2390.2112047118` | 1.000000000000000 |

## Retraction of 2026-10-29, in full

An earlier version of this receipt stated: *"for every physically conceivable number of variables
this algorithm is worse than exhaustive search, and it only becomes subexponential in fact in a
regime that does not exist in the universe."* That sentence carries two independent errors.

**First, the inference from the bound to the running time is invalid.** Their statement gives an
**upper** bound, `T(n) <= C * (bits+1)^d * 2^(A * runtimeScale(n))`. From that ceiling sitting above
`2^n` it does **not** follow that `T(n) > 2^n`: the algorithm may run far below its own bound.
Claiming it is worse than exhaustive search would require a **lower** bound, which nobody has. I had
conflated "the published bound is weak in this range" with "the algorithm is slow in this range".

**Second, "for every number of variables" is false.** The ratio crosses 1 **twice**, and the first
crossing is at `n = 49.175096`: from `n = 1` to `n = 49` the ratio is already **below** 1
(`n=1` gives 0.644; `n=8` gives 0.750; `n=49` gives 0.9994; `n=50` gives 1.0027). Between the two
crossings the ratio is above 1; outside them, below.

**What can be claimed, and only this:** with `A = 1`, the **published upper bound** sits above `2^n`
for `n` between about 50 and about `10^2390.21`, and below it outside that range. That is a
statement about the ceiling, not about running time, and it neither establishes nor excludes
practical usefulness.

**Why none of the self-tests could have caught either error.** They are a different class from a
mistranscription, which an arithmetic gate does catch. These are an error of **inference**, from the
ceiling to the time, and one of **quantification**, the "every n"; numerical checking is blind to
both by construction. The second had a structural cause: the script's bisection starts its search
**at the maximum**, so by design it can only find the descending branch, and the two crossings are
the two real branches of the same function. A self-test leg now checks both against the closed form.

**None of this bears on the refutation.** The ETH is asymptotic: `2^(o(n))` contradicts it formally
wherever the crossing falls, provided the two unestablished conditions above hold. The crossing is
the difference between "the bound is subexponential" and "the bound is subexponential and that
changes something at a scale anyone will run".

**And the published crossing is a lower bound on the real one.** The constant `A` is **existential**
in the statement, with no value given, and beating `2^n` requires `ratio < 1/A`. The numbers above
are for `A = 1`; any `A > 1` pushes the crossing much further out, and `A = 10` would take it to
`n ~ 10^2566976.50`. Below `A = e^2/16`, about 0.46, the exponential factor stays under `2^n` for
every `n` at least 1; at `n = 0` the exponent is `A * runtimeScale(0) = 0`, so both sides equal 1
and neither is below the other. Nothing here asserts a value for `A`, and the statement does not
give one.
